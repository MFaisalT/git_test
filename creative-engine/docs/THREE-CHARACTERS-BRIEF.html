Vince Velvet, Felix Faux & Mansour

Three original AI characters built around one commercial idea each: Vince makes people want to create, Felix teaches them how, and Mansour carries brands. This brief adds to the existing creative engine and Uncle Verdict pilot. It does not replace them. The voice lock, locked-plate keyframes, interaction rules and cold-viewer check all carry over.

Prepared by four specialist review roles plus two research agentsNo credits spent · nothing published · no one contacted

Evidence labels used throughout: verified tested or read at source in this project · reported third-party or platform listing, not tested · hypothesis a threshold or number to test · untested a production proposal not yet tried · assumption made to keep moving, owner can overrule.

The recommendation in five lines

  1. Build all three bibles now. Launch two accounts in the pilot: Vince and Felix. Mansour gets his bible, his locked voice and one unpublished proof episode, then launches after a cultural review.
  2. Vince and Felix work as a pair. Vince's spectacle creates the "how did they make that?" question. Felix answers it, including with Vince's failed takes. Failures become free content, not wasted credits.
  3. Dance comes from real motion, not from prompts. A licensed dancer's reference clip is transferred onto the character. Prompt-only dancing is the fallback and gets tested once, side by side.
  4. Credits are the real bottleneck. At 440 credits, the 14-day plan covers two newly generated pilots (Vince and Felix), one dance technique test, Mansour's reference pack, and four more posts cut from footage we already have. More volume needs a credit decision, not more planning.
  5. Imitate, then mutate. We copy the mechanics that made the references boom (the burst in a mundane place, divisive facial hair, a name-based catchphrase). We don't copy their footage, exact phrases or look-alike faces. No meme coins, fake fans or fake testimonials.

Owner direction (8 Oct): imitate, then mutate. Copying proven mechanics is the fastest way to learn, so we start close to what boomed and move away only as our own data tells us what works. We borrow ideas, structures, archetypes and the kind of trait (divisive facial hair, a name-based catchphrase, a burst of movement in a mundane place). None of that is ownable.

Where imitation stops paying off

  • Their footage and their routines. We never use their clips as motion references or re-cut them. That is their copyright, and both platforms push down reposted or lightly edited content in recommendations reported, re-check current wording. Generic moves (shadowboxing, a shuffle, a spin) are free to use.
  • The exact phrase or name. "Bumford Business" or "Jean Phil" with one letter changed invites a trademark or passing-off complaint and an impersonation report. Platforms remove accounts for impersonation; the reach goes with them. A name-based phrase of our own carries the same mechanic without that risk.
  • A look-alike. If someone can mistake our character for theirs in one frame, comments become "fake Jean Phil". That caps the account, and it costs us most with Mansour, whose value is to brands.
  • The meme coin. Out, as before.

A legitimate shortcut to their audience: the rival move. Reportedly a character called "Archibald Brown" grew by challenging Jean Phil to a fight. Vince could "challenge" Jean Phil to a dance-off with a duet or stitch. That uses the platform's own feature and borrows the attention honestly. It is a public interaction with another creator, so it needs your approval before it happens.

Why they do not overlap

  • Sound, face and pace differ by design. Vince is music and movement. Felix is speech and screens. Mansour is dialogue and warmth. Someone who sees all three in one feed should never mistake one for another.
  • One job each. Only Felix teaches. Vince shows a "how it's made" reveal at most once every four posts, then points to Felix's account and the affiliate link. Mansour never mentions how he is made beyond the required AI label. His commercial value depends on staying inside his world.
  • Planned cross-traffic. Felix dissects Vince's outtakes ("this is why his foot slid"). That sends curious viewers from the spectacle to the method. Mansour stays separate so brands get a clean context.
  • Separate risk. If Felix's opinions draw heat, it stays on his account and does not touch Mansour's brand safety.

B. Character bibles

Names and handles still need a clearance check (search the handles on both platforms and do a quick trademark search) before any account is created. The traits marked "locked" go into each character's bible in the engine. Changing them later means a new character, following the same rule as the voice lock.

Vince Velvet

A self-declared world-famous street maestro hears music no one else can hear and performs uninvited, fully committed "commissioned works" in places that never asked for them.

Visual identity
Mid-40s, very tall and lanky with long arms and legs (they read well in dance). Mutton chops: thick silver Victorian sideburns joined to the moustache, chin shaved clean. Divisive on purpose; it is his one-frame identifier. A silver pompadour that never moves. Heavy expressive black eyebrows doing most of the acting. Natural teeth and weathered skin with visible pores, not a glossy model face.
Personality
Grandly courteous, completely sincere, thinks he is famous ("my public"). Never admits a mistake: an awkward move is "avant-garde". Generous with compliments to objects.
Comic contradiction
He is sometimes brilliant and sometimes awful, and cannot tell which is which. He is proudest of the terrible moves and shrugs off the beautiful ones.
Voice & accent
Rarely speaks. When he does: a slow, warm mid-Atlantic baritone, theatrical, one to three words ("Maestro." "Available."). He gets a custom voice designed from the character and locked under the voice rule. Speaking so little keeps lip-sync risk and cost low.
Signature mannerisms
(0) Mundane act first (ordering a coffee, waiting at a lift), then the burst. (1) Taps a brass tuning fork on something in the room, holds it to his ear and nods: the beat starts. (2) Two finger snaps toward an invisible DJ: "Maestro." (3) A deep bow to an object (a palm, a vending machine, a bollard). (4) Gives the object a business card that reads only "VINCE VELVET — AVAILABLE."
Wardrobe (locked)
Crushed burgundy velvet tuxedo jacket with black satin lapels. Black trousers cut 5 cm too short. White sports socks. Gold loafers. A black bow tie that is always slightly crooked. One red carnation that never falls off, whatever happens.
Recurring settings
Grand hotel lobbies, museum atriums, a laundromat, a car park, train concourses, a supermarket aisle, a wedding venue after hours. All generic and unbranded.
Must stay consistent
Face and eyebrows, the mutton chops, the pompadour, the burgundy velvet, the too-short trousers with white socks, the carnation, the tuning-fork ritual, the locked voice, the #VelvetHours tag.
Imitated, then mutated
From Jean Phil: the mundane-then-burst structure, the formal outfit in the wrong place, divisive facial hair (mutton chops instead of a curled moustache). From Milton: a name-based catchphrase and hashtag ("Velvet Hours", used when he performs). His own additions are the style roulette and the excellence surprise.

Felix Faux

An openly fake, fast-talking AI man exposes exactly how he is made, insults bad AI craft, and gives away one real technique per video.

Visual identity
Early 30s. Bleached buzzcut with dark roots. Slightly crooked nose, freckles, a small scar through the left eyebrow. One silver hoop in his left ear, which is his deliberate "drift detector". Natural skin texture, never airbrushed.
Personality
Quick, blunt, cheerfully rude about bad work, unexpectedly generous with real knowledge. He treats the audience as smart.
Comic contradiction
He calls himself superior to humans, yet he credits "the human" for 40% of every video and can't stop pointing out his own flaws. A product that despises low-quality AI output, and is AI himself.
Voice & accent
Fast, dry, clipped. Neutral international English with a slight British edge and a slightly raspy texture. Speaking rate stays under the engine's 2.8 words-per-second ceiling so captions stay readable. Custom voice, locked.
Signature mannerisms
(1) Snaps his fingers to "regenerate" (a hard cut to a new place or outfit). (2) Taps his cheek: "Still me." (3) Two coloured stamps appear on screen: BIT (a joke or fiction) and FACT (a technique that is true and reproducible). This keeps his self-aware fiction separate from his technical claims. (4) Signs off with "I'm not real. The method is."
Wardrobe (locked)
Oversized black hoodie with a small embroidered "#0417" (his "seed") on the chest. Grey work trousers. White sneakers worn grey.
Recurring settings
The Grey Room: a matte grey void studio with one ring light, a rolling whiteboard and a monitor. Plus any absurd location he "regenerates" into to prove a point.
Must stay consistent
Face, the scar, hair and roots, the hoop in the left ear, the "#0417" hoodie, the voice, the BIT/FACT system.

Mansour Al-Hafawa

A Riyadh gentleman who cannot stop hosting turns every hotel lobby, supermarket and stranger into a guest he must honour, and once in a while reveals a genuinely beautiful ballroom talent.

Name
"Hafawa" (حفاوة) means a warm welcome. It is a stage name, not a real family name. A Saudi reviewer must confirm it reads as charming rather than odd. No "Abu" nickname, to keep clear of the Abu Shalab reference.
Visual identity
Early 50s. Broad shoulders and a round belly. A neatly trimmed salt-and-pepper beard joined to a sharp moustache. Thick brows, large expressive eyes, deep laugh lines. His head is always covered by shemagh and agal, so his silhouette is the red-and-white headdress, never a haircut.
Personality
Generous to the point of aggression, proud, sentimental, easily offended on behalf of others' comfort, secretly afraid of only one person: his wife.
Comic contradiction
He insists he is "a very simple, quiet man" while being the loudest and most extravagant presence in every room. Fully traditional in dress and manners, and secretly a superb Viennese-waltz and tap dancer. He will not explain where he learned it.
Voice & accent
A warm, booming baritone. English with a Najdi (central Saudi) colour: some /p/ sounds lean toward /b/, a tapped "r", lengthened vowels for emphasis. Everyday Gulf expressions with on-screen translation the first time each appears: "yalla" (come on), "zain" (good), "ya rajjal" (oh man). He avoids religious oaths and expressions as punchlines. Intelligibility comes first: if a cold viewer can't transcribe a line, the accent is too heavy. Custom voice designed from the character, not imitated from any real person, and locked.
Signature mannerisms
(1) "No no no. I insist." with a raised palm. (2) Pours Arabic coffee from his own dallah, which he brings everywhere: dallah in the left hand, cup offered with the right. (3) Points with his camel stick (a thin traditional riding cane, the mishab). (4) Looks into the lens and whispers "Turki, film this" to the nephew filming him.
Wardrobe (locked)
Thobe: off-white in summer episodes, charcoal wool in winter episodes (a real seasonal practice, and visually different from the white-thobe reference). Red-and-white shemagh in a fixed fold, black agal, brown leather sandals. A black bisht with gold trim appears only when he considers the mission "official", which works as a visual sign that things are escalating. Camel stick.
Recurring settings
His majlis at home (floor cushions, incense burner, dallah set), generic five-star lobbies, a supermarket, airport arrivals, a winter desert camp. All unbranded until a sponsor is involved.
Recurring relationships (all off-screen or voice-only, which also saves generation cost)
Turki, his long-suffering nephew filming every episode; heard sighing, his hand sometimes in shot. Umm Fahad, his wife, heard only through voice notes; the one force that stops him mid-move. Abu Saleh, the neighbour whose majlis is "slightly bigger"; never seen, often referenced.
Must stay consistent
Face, beard shape, headdress fold and pattern, sandals, camel stick, dallah, the locked voice and accent level, the cast of three off-screen characters.
Cultural guardrails
The comedy comes from excess of a real virtue (hospitality and karam), never from mocking Saudi people, Islam or religious practice. No prayer, mosque or religious-object gags. No "rich sheikh" money jokes. Mansour is never the punchline of a nationality joke; the situation is. A Saudi reviewer reads every script before it is produced assumption: owner can source one.

Dance direction: specific styles, not "Saudi" or "Western"

  • Najdi ʿArḍah-inspired steps: a dignified, upright side-step with his stick raised in place of a sword, to a frame-drum rhythm. He performs it straight and proudly; the comedy comes from the context (a hotel corridor with a room-service trolley as his "drum section"). The cultural reviewer must approve each use, because the ʿArḍah is a national heritage dance.
  • Mizmar-inspired stick work (the Hijazi stick dance): rhythmic stick twirls and turns, with his camel stick.
  • Viennese waltz, his surprise talent: continuous natural and reverse turns, rise and fall, arms framed around an invisible partner, 3/4 time at about 58–60 bars per minute. The bisht flares on the turns.
  • Rhythm tap: shuffles, flaps and a time step, in tap shoes he reveals under the thobe hem.
  • Not used: dabke. It is Levantine, not Saudi, and using it would be careless.

C. Pilot content

Nine pilot concepts

Vince

  1. Style Roulette: The Grand Lobby developed below. Minuet, krump, flamenco footwork, then sudden ballet excellence, then a wobble and a bow to a potted palm.
  2. The Commission: Revolving Door. A tango with a revolving door. Each time it turns, he has a new "partner" (the empty segment). He gets dizzy, exits on the wrong side, bows to the door.
  3. Behind the Velvet #1. A split screen of the dancer's reference clip and Vince, then three failed takes (a sliding foot, a melted carnation). His line: "The city plays. I just use the right tools."

Felix

  1. Hot Take: "Four faces, one man" developed below. He has changed faces four times in the video, and the viewer is dared to rewatch. Then the reference-pack fix.
  2. Autopsy #1: The Dial That Lied. Uncle Verdict's real doubled "1" and the needle turning the wrong way, then the cropped-still fix ("never regenerate a closer shot; crop the approved frame"). Footage already exists, so near-zero credits.

Mansour

  1. The Inspection: Five-Star Lobby developed below. He serves the concierge Arabic coffee until the concierge's hand shakes, then waltzes to the lift.

E. Monetization design

Vince: spectacle to affiliate

Program facts reported. These come from search summaries of higgsfield.ai/affiliate and the Higgsfield blog; the pages themselves could not be opened from here:

  • Commission is up to 25% of plan revenue, tiered, recurring for up to 12 months per referral. Monthly plans pay at each renewal; quarterly and annual plans are credited upfront. Refunds earn nothing.
  • Referred users get up to 50% off, valid for 3 hours after logging in through the link.
  • Prohibited: paid ads, bidding on the Higgsfield brand name, coupon and cashback sites. An older ambassador page says "up to 15%" (Impact.com) and requires promoters to identify as an independent affiliate. The two pages conflict.
  • Still unknown: the attribution (cookie) window, payout threshold and method, whether sub-IDs per post are supported, and the full terms page. All of these must be read in the dashboard after joining, which is an owner action.

Conversion path

  1. Spectacle (Style Roulette, The Commission) creates the question "how is this made?". No selling in these posts.
  2. Behind the Velvet (1 post in 4) answers it. It shows the real dancer's clip next to Vince, then the failed takes, then the tool. The "#ad · affiliate link" disclosure appears on screen and in Vince's spoken line, and the platform's commercial-content toggle is on. A disclosure only in the caption is not enough for the FTC.
  3. Link: on Instagram, a bio link to a simple link page (clearly "Vince Velvet's links", never styled to look like Higgsfield) plus a story link sticker. TikTok links in bio require 1,000 followers, so on TikTok the reveal posts point viewers to Instagram or to Felix's site until then.
  4. Deeper intent goes to Felix: "the full method is on Felix's account". The course also links the tool honestly, with disclosure.
  5. The 3-hour discount is a real, fair urgency lever, if the dashboard confirms it is still offered: "50% off for 3 hours after you tap".

Attribution plan

  • Use one affiliate link per platform and series if the program supports sub-IDs. If not, the link page records which post a click came from (a short code per post) before handing off to the affiliate link.
  • Log each post's views, link clicks, and the dashboard's sign-ups and revenue weekly. Sign-ups usually lag clicks by days, so judge on a 14-day window, not per post.
  • Economics (formula only, no invented prices): affiliate revenue ≈ views × link-click rate × sign-up rate × average plan revenue × commission rate. Vince only pays off if this beats roughly 250–350 credits per spectacle episode. The pilot measures the first two rates; the rest come from the dashboard.

Felix: the course

Target learner
The "stuck creator". They have made AI clips and can't keep a character consistent across episodes, so their face drifts and the voice changes. They want a recurring character for their own channel or for clients. Not complete beginners; not developers.
Course outcome
In 21 days, build one original AI character and publish three finished 15–30 s episodes with the same face, the same voice and the same world. Measured by the learner's own three exports, passing the course's QA checklist.
Minimum viable curriculum
  1. Character before face: premise, contradiction, a format that can generate new episodes. The bible template.
  2. The reference pack: the five-image method, prop references, what "locked" means.
  3. Voice: design a custom voice from the character, pick a take, clone it, lock it, and keep it identical across shots.
  4. Shot planning: timed beats, one movement per shot, naming the lens in plain words, speech rate, start and end keyframes.
  5. Generate and repair: the locked-plate crop, hand-object interactions, motion transfer from licensed movement, and a library of real failures with what each repair cost.
  6. Edit, test, disclose: replacing a line with the locked voice, cutting on the beat, the cold-viewer test, AI labels versus ad disclosure.
Included resources
Bible template, shot-prompt template, QA checklist, the failure library (real before-and-after clips with credit costs), a credit budget sheet, a disclosure checklist, and the reference pack for Felix himself to practise with.
Differentiation
Taught from a logged production record, not a tool list. Every lesson comes from a real failure in this lab (the doubled dial numeral, the drifting voice, the wrong-way needle), with what the fix cost. It is honest about paid tools, about human work, and about what still fails. The free content teaches complete techniques; the paid course adds the organised sequence, templates, the failure library and, at the higher tier, feedback.
Pricing hypothesis
hypothesis $79 self-paced. $199 with two recorded critiques of the learner's episodes. Founding pre-sale at $49 for the first 30 learners, with a refund promise if the course does not ship by the stated date. These numbers are a starting point to test, not market research.
Demand test before building
  1. Free lesson (the five-image pack plus the QA checklist) on a one-page site, in exchange for an email, with one question: "Describe the character you want to build."
  2. Waitlist conversion is measured for 14 days from Felix's posts. A spoken, memorable URL works around TikTok's link limit.
  3. Gate: at hypothesis ≥ 25 waitlist sign-ups, open a founding pre-sale to the waitlist only.
  4. Build the full course only after ≥ 10 pre-sales. Until then, only the free lesson and module 1 exist.
